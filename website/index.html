New in 0.2

0.2 cannot message 0.1. Update, and ask your contacts to update too — anyone still on 0.1 is told, and your messages to them wait until they do.

🛰️ The relay is optional 0.2

Keep the Night Drop relay, add up to four of your own, remove ours, or use direct only with no relay at all — then nothing about your messages ever touches a server. With a relay, each chat has its own mailbox under an address that changes daily, so the relay can't link messages from different contacts.

📲 Pair with no server 0.2

Your QR code carries a one-time pass, good for a day, that lets one person send you one chat request — so pairing works on direct only too. You still decide whether to accept.

🚫 Screenshots blocked by default 0.2

On Android, screenshots and screen recordings of Night Drop come out black. If your contact blocks them, they're blocked in your chat with them too. You can still allow screenshots in one chat — to keep evidence of abuse, say — and they're told. With blocking off, a screenshot or a recording is reported to the other person.

🌫️ Cover traffic on by default 0.2

New identities that use a relay send decoy traffic, so the relay can't read your activity from the volume and timing of your mail. You can turn it off to save battery.

🔗 Invites without the clipboard 0.2

Share an invite straight to the app you choose; a copied link is marked sensitive and cleared after a minute. After a network switch, the app says it's reconnecting to Tor instead of leaving you guessing.

🛡️ Tighter everywhere 0.2

Signed update notices that expire, daily limits on what one contact can send, no app data in Android backups, keyboards that can't learn what you type, a growing wait after wrong lock codes, and a Windows download. The full list is in the release notes.

Built privacy-first, from the ground up

🕵️ Anonymous by default

No sign-up, no email, no phone number. Your identity is a key that lives only on your device. The default name is just “Anon”.

🔒 End-to-end encrypted

Every message uses the Signal Double Ratchet. Only you and the person you’re talking to can read it — with forward secrecy on every message.

🧅 Peer-to-peer over Tor

Messages travel directly between devices through the Tor network. There’s no central server watching who talks to whom.

📵 No server-side keys, no logs

We can’t read your messages because we never hold your keys — and we keep no logs. There’s nothing to hand over, because there’s nothing to keep.

📱 Local-first storage

By default your history stays on your device, encrypted at rest. Optionally store it on the server for 24 hours to save space — and both of you are clearly warned while that’s on.

🛂 You approve who reaches you

Pair by QR code or a short code with a shared secret. A stranger can’t message you until you approve the request.

📮 Delivered even when they’re offline

If your contact is offline, the encrypted message waits on an untrusted relay for up to 24 hours — it sees only ciphertext, never content. On direct only, it waits on your phone until you're both online. You see when it’s queued and when it’s delivered.

🛰️ Your relays, or none

Add up to four relays that hold your mailbox. Messages are copied to all of them, so one relay being down or blocked doesn’t drop your mail — and every relay still sees only ciphertext. Run your own, remove ours, or use none at all; nothing about a relay is trusted.

📷 Photos and videos, encrypted too

Attachments travel end-to-end encrypted like every message, and are stored sealed on your device. Before anything is sent, the location, camera model and time the phone wrote into a photo or video are removed, and images are compressed on-device so they move fast over Tor.

🗝️ Backups you own

Optional encrypted backups protected by a recovery password shown to you exactly once — never stored, never sent anywhere. Lose the password, lose the backup: that’s the point. Restore keeps your identity and chats.

🎚️ Back up your way

Choose Lite (just your identity and contacts, no messages) or Full (with history and media) — save it to a file, or opt in to an encrypted 24-hour copy on the server that only your password can open. You can even back up a single chat and merge it back later.

⏱️ Disappearing messages

Set a per-chat timer — an hour, a day, a week — and messages sent from then on delete themselves on both devices once they are that old. It’s a shared setting, so changing it keeps both sides in sync.

↩️ Unsend and edit

Change your mind? Edit a message or delete it for everyone. If it hasn’t reached them yet it’s pulled back before it ever arrives; if it has, it turns into a “deleted” marker on both sides.

🫥 Nothing hidden between you

When the other person keeps a backup of your chat, or turns on 24-hour server storage, you see a clear, persistent warning. Privacy you can’t see isn’t privacy — so the app tells you where your messages live.

🔕 Notifications without leaks

New-message alerts are generated on your device and say only “New message” — no content, no sender, and nothing goes through a push provider tied to your identity. On Android you can opt in to keep receiving them in the background over Tor — still with no push provider.

🧹 Leave without a trace

Delete a chat and the other person is told; log out and your identity, keys, messages, and decrypted files are wiped from the device. Nothing lingers on a server, because nothing was there.

🚫 Screenshots blocked

On Android, screenshots and screen recording are blocked by default, for you and, in your chat, for your contact. Turn it off and each capture is reported to the other person instead. A camera pointed at the screen gets past any app — the honest limits page says what else does.

🔥 Burn after reading

Send a message or photo that stays hidden until it's opened, then deletes itself on a timer. Unopened, it's gone after 24 hours.

🔢 Check who you're talking to

Compare a safety number with your contact in person or on a call, or scan each other's code. If they match, no one is in the middle of your chat.

🔐 App lock and wipe code

Lock the app with a code of its own. Set a second code that, entered instead, wipes your identity and messages rather than unlocking.

🌉 Works where Tor is blocked

Tor bridges, including WebTunnel, which carries Tor inside ordinary HTTPS. The app can fetch bridges from the Tor Project for you — the one request that doesn't go through Tor, made only when you agree.

💻 One app, every device

Android, Windows and Linux from one codebase — with the same Rust security core doing the cryptography on every platform.

Why Night Drop is different

Most “private” messengers still tie you to a phone number, keep metadata on their servers, or hold keys that can be compelled. Night Drop is designed so there is simply nothing to collect.

Night Drop

  • ✅ No phone number or account
  • ✅ No server-side keys
  • ✅ No logs, minimal metadata
  • ✅ Peer-to-peer over Tor; the relay is optional
  • ✅ Local-first; optional 24h server storage with a warning
  • ✅ You approve contacts before the first message
  • ✅ Encrypted backups only you can open — Lite, Full, or one chat
  • ✅ Disappearing messages and delete-for-everyone
  • ✅ Screenshots and screen recording blocked by default (Android)
  • ✅ Reproducible builds, checked by F-Droid
  • ✅ Clear warnings when a message is stored or backed up
  • ✅ Donations with no account — Monero, Zcash, or Bitcoin silent payments

Typical “secure” chat apps

  • ⚠️ Require a phone number that identifies you
  • ⚠️ Keep accounts and metadata on central servers
  • ⚠️ Hold keys or routing data that can be compelled
  • ⚠️ Connect over the open internet by default
  • ⚠️ Store history in the cloud, sometimes by default
  • ⚠️ Anyone with your number can reach you
  • ⚠️ Cloud backups the provider can open or be compelled to share
  • ⚠️ Quietly sync or store data without telling you
  • ⚠️ Paid tiers tied to your identity

Android Windows Linux

The Android button opens the list of builds so you can pick one. The universal build runs on any phone; the per-processor builds are roughly a third the size, and arm64-v8a suits almost every phone made since 2015. If you install a per-processor build you can't switch back to the universal one without uninstalling first, because Android reads it as a downgrade.

The Windows installer (Windows 10 or later, 64-bit) needs no administrator rights. It is not code-signed yet, so Windows may warn about an unknown publisher; check it against the signed SHA256SUMS first (see SECURITY.md).
