What we can't protect you from

Every privacy tool has limits. A tool that hides them isn't one you should trust. Here are ours, in plain language — so you can decide what Night Drop is right for, and what it isn't.

Night Drop is built so that we can't read your messages, map who you talk to, or hand over data we never keep. That part is structural — it holds even if we're compelled. But no messenger can protect you from everything. The honest boundaries below fall into three groups: things that are out of scope by design, things only you can control, and where the app is still being hardened.

Out of scope — by design

These aren't bugs to be fixed; they're the edges of what any software of this kind can promise. We'd rather say so than pretend otherwise.

🦠 A compromised device by design

If malware or spyware with deep access is on your phone or computer, it can read what you type and see on screen before Night Drop ever encrypts it. No app can defend against a fully compromised device from inside that device. Keep your device updated and uncompromised — that's the foundation everything else rests on.

🌐 An adversary who can defeat Tor itself by design

Night Drop routes over Tor to hide the network path. A powerful global observer who can watch huge portions of the internet at once might, in theory, correlate traffic timing and volume to link the two ends of a conversation. We pad message sizes to blunt this, and cover traffic (on by default for new identities that use a relay) blurs your activity as a relay sees it — but defeating a global passive adversary is an open problem for Tor itself, not something we can solve alone.

🔑 Losing your identity with no backup by design

Your identity lives only on your device — that's the anonymity guarantee. If you lose the device and never made a backup, those chats are gone, and no one (including us) can recover them. Night Drop offers optional encrypted backups so you can choose recoverability; the default is that there's nothing to recover.

🕳️ The fact that you use Tor by design

Night Drop hides what you send and who you send it to. It doesn't hide from your own network that you're using Tor at all. In places where using Tor is itself risky, that's a separate consideration — Tor bridges, including WebTunnel, help; their own limits are below.

Only you can control this

Some things are simply outside any app's reach, because they live on the other person's side or in your own choices.

🧑‍🤝‍🧑 The person you're talking to

End-to-end encryption means only the two of you can read a message. It can't stop the other person from taking a screenshot, keeping their own backup, or showing someone their screen. When they turn on server storage or keep a backup of your chat, Night Drop shows you a clear, persistent warning — because privacy you can't see isn't privacy. But trust between two people is still between two people.

On screenshots specifically: on Android, Night Drop blocks screenshots and screen recording by default, and if the other person blocks them, they are blocked in your chat with them too. You can still allow screenshots in one chat — to keep evidence of abuse, say — and they are told. When blocking is off, you're told when the other person screenshots your chat (Android 14 and later) or records their screen (Android 15 and later). Read the limits carefully: no app can block or detect a camera pointed at the screen, a modified copy of the app can ignore the block, desktop computers can neither block nor detect captures, and older Android detects nothing. No notice is not evidence that nothing was captured.

🗝️ Your backup password

Encrypted backups are protected by a recovery password shown to you exactly once and never stored anywhere. That's what keeps a backup safe from us and everyone else — and it means if you lose the password, you lose the backup. We can't reset it, because we never had it. Write it down somewhere safe.

👀 Someone watching over your shoulder

Disappearing messages and delete-for-everyone remove content from both devices, but they can't un-see what someone already read, photographed with another camera, or remembered. Physical-world observation is always outside the software.

🔒 Night Drop inside Samsung Secure Folder

Secure Folder is a separate user account, and Android freezes the apps inside it while it's locked. A copy of Night Drop kept there isn't running, so it receives nothing until you unlock the folder and open it — the sender sees the message held, not delivered, however long that takes. Turning on background delivery inside the app can't change this.

Your messages are no less private there — arguably more so, since nothing reaches the lock screen. What you give up is knowing when someone has written to you.

Where we're still hardening

Night Drop is open source and built on audited cryptography libraries, but the app itself is young. We'd rather you know exactly where it stands than trust a superlative.

Not yet independently audited

The cryptography is built on well-reviewed libraries (the same Double Ratchet family used by Signal, the Tor project's own Rust implementation, and vetted password and encryption primitives), and the security-critical code is isolated in a single Rust core. But the app as a whole has not yet had an independent security audit. Until it has, we won't claim more than that — and we'll publish the audit and its fixes when it's done. Treat Night Drop as promising and improving, not battle-tested.

A seized phone shows that you use Night Drop, and how many contacts you have

Since 0.1.16 your own Tor address and your contacts' addresses are kept in Night Drop's encrypted store, with your messages and behind the app lock if you set one. Someone who takes your phone and copies its storage can still see that Night Drop is installed and runs a Tor onion service, and one small file per contact holding that contact's public key — so how many contacts you have, and, to anyone who also has one of their phones, that the two of you talk.

Deleting a chat removes that contact's file, and logging out or using the wipe code clears them all. A short lock code can be guessed by someone with a copy and time, so a longer one is better.

What a relay can still link about your offline messages

Messages sent while you are offline wait on a relay. From 0.1.26 each chat drops them in its own mailbox, under an address that changes every day, and your phone collects them in small groups over separate Tor connections, padded with mailboxes that do not exist. So a relay can no longer tell that messages from different people, or from different days, are for the same person.

What it can still tell: that messages in one chat on one day belong together; which mailboxes your phone collects as one group (about seven contacts at most); that a mailbox checked for weeks and never used is probably padding; and a burst when you send to several people at once. A chat whose other side runs a version before 0.1.26 keeps the old, fixed address in both directions until they update, and you will see a notice in that chat. The limit of 50 contacts is what keeps those groups small; it is enforced by the app, not the protocol. And offline messages rely on your clock: if it is wrong by more than three hours, a message can go to a mailbox nobody checks and expire unread after 24 hours. Messages delivered directly are unaffected.

“Direct only” tells your contacts when you come online.

From 0.2 you can use Night Drop with no relay at all. Then nothing about your messages ever touches a server — but messages to you wait on your contacts' phones and are sent the moment your app is online, so each contact can tell, roughly, when you come online. With a relay, messages wait there instead. Opening the app does not announce anything by itself.

It also means two people on direct only must be online at the same time for a message to arrive. It waits on the sender's phone until then, with no 24-hour limit.

Your QR code lets one person knock, for one day.

So that pairing needs no server, your invite carries a key that lets whoever has it reach your phone even once it only accepts your contacts. That gets their chat request to you, and nothing else: you still decide whether to accept it, and until you do they learn nothing about you. The key stops working once one request has used it, or after a day. So a photo of your QR code posted online lets at most one stranger send you a request you can decline.

Bridges beat a blocked relay list. Traffic inspection is tested, not proven.

If your network blocks Tor by blocking the public list of relays, a bridge is an unlisted way in. If it instead inspects traffic and blocks Tor by how it looks, that needs a WebTunnel bridge — Tor carried inside ordinary HTTPS to a normal-looking web server, with a TLS handshake matched to Chrome's.

What we have measured, on our own network: with every route except that one web server blocked at the router, Night Drop still connected and published its address; with the bridge removed under the same block, it could not build a single circuit. An intrusion-detection system carrying 52,311 public signatures, over a thousand of them for Tor, raised nothing and saw only ordinary TLS.

What we have not done is run it from inside a country that filters this way. Public tooling is a much weaker adversary than a national firewall, and one thing is not disguised at all: the shape of the traffic. A connection that pulls tens of megabytes while sending very little does not look like reading a web page, however ordinary the handshake is. If being identified as a Tor user is itself dangerous where you are, do not rely on this alone.

“Get bridges” does not go through Tor.

From 0.1.28 the bridges screen can ask the Tor Project for WebTunnel bridges. That one request has to work when Tor cannot, so it goes directly, disguised as a connection to a content-delivery network. That network and the Tor Project see your IP address — the Tor Project uses it to pick bridges for your country.

The request contains nothing about you, and nothing is sent to Night Drop. It only runs when you tap the button and agree, and nothing is saved until you save the bridges yourself. If being seen contacting the Tor Project is itself dangerous where you are, get bridges another way.

🧪 Real-world Tor testing is ongoing in progress

The full messaging flow is verified end to end in automated tests, and the Tor path has been exercised over the live network. Broad testing across many real devices, networks, and conditions is still underway. Expect rough edges on flaky connections.

🛰️ Relays are optional 0.2

When both people are online, messages go directly, device to device. A relay holds sealed, unreadable messages for up to 24 hours while you are offline, and makes short codes possible. The Night Drop relay is the default; you can add your own (up to four), remove ours, or use direct only with no relay at all — then you receive messages only while the app is online, and add contacts by QR code only.

🎞️ Opening an attachment in another app in progress

Photos and videos are stored sealed on your device. To play a video in your phone's built-in player, we briefly write a decrypted copy to an app-private, owner-only folder — other apps can't read it — and wipe that folder on logout and on the next launch. While the external player has the file open, and until that cleanup runs, a decrypted copy exists on disk. Viewing an image in-app never writes plaintext out.

🔮 Long-horizon quantum computing tracked

The part of the encryption that protects message contents already has a strong margin against future quantum computers. Pairing by short code already mixes in a post-quantum key exchange (ML-KEM-768), but the handshake that starts each chat's encryption is classical today, like most messengers — there's no live threat, but a well-resourced adversary could record traffic now to try to decrypt it years from now. Our plan for closing that gap is documented publicly rather than glossed over.

So what can you count on?

Within those honest limits, Night Drop is designed so that we hold no key to your messages, keep no logs, and store no account that could be seized or subpoenaed. Any server only ever sees sealed, unreadable blobs under short-lived, unlinkable handles. You have no phone number or email tied to your identity, you approve who can reach you, and your history lives on your device by default. That's the deposit box: sealed, dropped, and opened by no one in between.
